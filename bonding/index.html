Chemical bonding and molecular shape

Why is salt a hard crystal and water a bent molecule? Atoms bond by moving or sharing their valence electrons. Count those electrons and you can predict how atoms connect, how strong the bonds are and what shape the molecule takes. Brown, Chapter 8, section 5.8 and sections 9.1–9.2.

After this week you should be able to
  • tell ionic, covalent and metallic bonding apart, and explain what makes a lattice energy large
  • use electronegativity to say how polar a bond is
  • draw Lewis structures, including resonance structures and exceptions to the octet rule
  • pick the best Lewis structure with formal charges, and tell formal charge, oxidation number and partial charge apart
  • estimate a reaction enthalpy from bond enthalpies
  • predict electron-domain and molecular geometry with VSEPR

1 · Ionic bonds

Bonds are made by the valence electrons, the electrons in the outermost shell (week 6). A Lewis symbol shows them as dots around the element symbol, at most two per side: sulfur, [Ne] 3s² 3p⁴, has six. Atoms tend to gain, lose or share electrons until they have eight valence electrons, the configuration of a noble gas: the octet rule. It works best for s and p electrons, and it has exceptions (section 3).

Ionic

A metal gives electrons to a nonmetal. The ions attract each other electrostatically. Example: Na⁺Cl⁻, an ionic solid.

Covalent

Two atoms share valence electrons. Example: H₂O, made of molecules.

Metallic

The valence electrons are shared by the whole solid. Example: silver, Ag(s).

Lattice energy: why ionic solids are so stable

Sodium gives up its 3s electron and becomes Na⁺ ([Ne]); chlorine takes it and becomes Cl⁻ ([Ar]). On its own that costs energy (496 kJ/mol to ionize Na, only 349 kJ/mol back from Cl). What pays for it is the attraction between the ions once they pack into a crystal. The lattice energy is the energy needed to pull one mole of the solid apart into gaseous ions:

\[ \mathrm{NaCl}(s) \rightarrow \mathrm{Na^+}(g) + \mathrm{Cl^-}(g) \qquad \Delta H_\text{lattice} = +788\ \mathrm{kJ/mol} \]

It is always positive (endothermic). How large it is follows from Coulomb's law: the attraction grows with the charges \(Q_1, Q_2\) of the ions and falls with their distance \(d\), the sum of the ionic radii.

\[ E_\text{el} = \kappa\,\frac{Q_1 Q_2}{d} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \Delta H_\text{lattice} \propto \frac{|Q_1 Q_2|}{d} \]

Lattice energies: Brown Table 8.1. \(d\) = sum of the ionic radii (Brown Fig. 7.8). Only salts with one cation per anion are shown, so the Coulomb factor compares like with like. Tap a point.

  1. Going down the halides (LiF → LiCl → LiI) the anion gets bigger, \(d\) grows, and the lattice energy drops.
  2. MgO, CaO and SrO have charges 2+ and 2−: \(|Q_1Q_2| = 4\). Their lattice energies are about four times larger. Charge matters more than size, because ionic radii only vary over a small range (problem set 7, question 3).

Getting the lattice energy from a cycle (Born–Haber)

The lattice energy cannot be measured directly. But enthalpy is a state function (week 4): go from the solid to the gaseous ions by a detour through steps you can measure, and the total is the same.

2 · Covalent bonds and polarity

Most substances are not ionic: the atoms share electron pairs. In H₂ each H brings one electron, the pair sits between the nuclei and attracts both: H–H. Each line in a Lewis structure is one shared pair. Atoms can share two pairs (a double bond, O=C=O) or three (a triple bond, N≡N). More shared pairs, shorter bonds: N–N 147 pm, N=N 124 pm, N≡N 110 pm.

Who gets the electrons? Electronegativity

Electronegativity is the ability of an atom in a molecule to attract electrons to itself (Pauling's scale, no units). It is high for atoms with a large ionization energy and a very negative electron affinity: it increases to the right and to the top of the periodic table. Fluorine (4.0) is the most electronegative element, caesium (0.7) the least.

Rule of thumb from the lecture: a difference of about 2 or more means the bond is ionic. It is a useful guide, not a law: there is no sharp line between ionic and covalent.

Dipole moments

Equal and opposite charges \(\pm Q\) a distance \(r\) apart form a dipole, with dipole moment \(\mu = Q\,r\), measured in debye: 1 D = 3.34 × 10⁻³⁰ C m. A proton and an electron 0.1 nm apart would give 4.8 D. Real bonds share the electrons only partly, so the partial charges \(\delta\) are smaller. You can work them out from the measured dipole moment and bond length:

\[ \delta = \frac{\mu}{e\, r} \]

Ionic or covalent? A continuum

The higher the oxidation number of a metal, the more covalent its bonds. Manganese(II) oxide, MnO, is an ionic solid that melts at 1842 °C. Manganese(VII) oxide, Mn₂O₇, is made of molecules and is a liquid at room temperature (it melts at 5.9 °C).

3 · Lewis structures

The recipe from the lecture:

  1. Add up the valence electrons of all atoms. Add one for each negative charge, take one away for each positive charge.
  2. Write the symbols and connect them with single bonds. The central atom is often written first in the formula (not always). H is never central.
  3. Complete the octets of the outer atoms. H only needs 2 electrons.
  4. Put any remaining electrons on the central atom, even if that gives it more than 8.
  5. If the central atom still has fewer than 8, turn lone pairs of its neighbours into multiple bonds.

    Formal charge: choosing between structures

    Sometimes several Lewis structures obey the octet rule. To decide, give each atom its formal charge:

    \[ \text{FC} = (\text{valence } e^-) - (\text{non-bonding } e^-) - \tfrac12(\text{bonding } e^-) \]

    The formal charges add up to the charge of the molecule or ion. The dominant structure has formal charges closest to zero, and puts any negative formal charge on the more electronegative atom.

    Resonance

    Ozone, O₃, needs one single and one double bond to the central O, and there are two equally good ways to draw that. Yet both O–O bonds are measured to be the same length. Neither structure alone is right: the real molecule is a blend of the two resonance structures, written with a double-headed arrow ⟷. The electrons of the double bond are spread over the whole molecule. The same happens in benzene, C₆H₆: its six C–C bonds are all equal, in between a single and a double bond. Pick O₃, NO₃⁻, CO₃²⁻ or HCO₂⁻ above and go to the last step.

    Exceptions to the octet rule

    Odd number of electrons

    NO has 11 valence electrons, so one is always unpaired. (Pick NO above.)

    Less than an octet

    Boron and beryllium compounds, for example BF₃: B keeps only 6 electrons.

    More than an octet

    Central atoms from period 3 on can hold 10 or 12 electrons: PCl₅, SF₆, XeF₄. These are called hypervalent. Period-2 atoms (C, N, O, F) never can.

    4 · Bond strength and length

    The bond enthalpy \(D\) is the enthalpy change for breaking one mole of a particular bond in the gas phase. It is always positive: breaking a bond costs energy. Tables give average values, because the same bond is a little different in different molecules (Lecture 7, Brown Table 8.3). That is enough to estimate a reaction enthalpy: pull all reactant molecules apart into atoms, then build the products.

    \[ \Delta H_\text{rxn} \approx \sum D(\text{bonds broken}) - \sum D(\text{bonds formed}) \]
    Reaction
    1. Problem set 7, question 2: 2 H₂ + O₂ → 2 H₂O. The estimate is for water vapour. Liquid water releases another 44 kJ/mol when it condenses, so the true value for H₂O(l) is more negative.
    2. Why is burning methane exothermic? Compare the energy needed to break C–H and O=O with the energy released by forming C=O and O–H.
    3. For which reaction is the estimate worst? Average bond enthalpies are averages over many molecules.

    Shorter bonds are stronger

    Between the same two atoms, every extra shared pair pulls the atoms closer together and makes the bond harder to break.

    5 · Molecular shape (VSEPR)

    A Lewis structure shows which atoms are connected, but not the shape of the molecule. CCl₄ is drawn flat, yet its four Cl atoms sit at the corners of a tetrahedron, all Cl–C–Cl angles 109.5°.

    The valence-shell electron-pair repulsion (VSEPR) model: the electron pairs around the central atom repel each other and get as far apart as possible, like balloons tied together. Each bond (single, double or triple) and each lone pair counts as one electron domain. The number of domains fixes the electron-domain geometry. The molecular geometry describes only where the atoms are: the lone pairs are there, but invisible.

    Bonding domains (atoms around the centre)
    Lone pairs on the central atom
    1. Four domains: go from 0 to 1 to 2 lone pairs. The electron-domain geometry stays tetrahedral, but the molecule goes from tetrahedral (CH₄) to trigonal pyramidal (NH₃) to bent (H₂O).
    2. Five domains: the lone pairs always take the equatorial positions, where they have the most room (only two neighbours at 90°). That gives seesaw, T-shaped and linear.
    3. Six domains with two lone pairs: they sit opposite each other, and the molecule is square planar (XeF₄, BrF₄⁻).
    Refinements

    Lone pairs take up more space than bonding pairs, and so do multiple bonds. They push the other bonds closer together: H–C–H 109.5° in CH₄, H–N–H 107° in NH₃, H–O–H 104.5° in H₂O. In phosgene, COCl₂, the C=O double bond pushes the Cl–C–Cl angle down to 111.4°.

    Shape decides polarity

    Bond dipoles add up like vectors. In CO₂ (linear) and CCl₄ (tetrahedral) they cancel, so the molecules are nonpolar although their bonds are polar. In H₂O (bent) and NH₃ (pyramidal) they do not: these molecules are polar. This matters a lot in topic 8.
