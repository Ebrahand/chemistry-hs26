Liquids and intermolecular forces

Why is water a liquid at room temperature but methane a gas? Why does water boil at 72 °C on Mount Everest? It all comes down to how strongly molecules pull on each other. Brown, Chapter 11.

1 · What holds a liquid together?

Inside a molecule, atoms are held together by chemical bonds (intramolecular forces, week 7). Between molecules there are much weaker attractions, the intermolecular forces. They decide whether a substance is a gas, a liquid or a solid at a given temperature, and how easily it boils, flows or evaporates.

How much weaker? Boiling 1 mol of water takes 40.7 kJ. Breaking all the O–H bonds in that mole would take about \(2 \times 463 = 926\) kJ. When water boils, the H₂O molecules stay intact. The bubbles are water vapour, not hydrogen and oxygen.

Attraction against motion

Every phase is a contest between two things: the attraction between molecules, which pulls them together, and their kinetic energy (set by the temperature, week 2), which shakes them apart. Play with both in this toy model.

  2. Raise the temperature slowly. More molecules escape into the vapour. Above a certain temperature the liquid is gone.
  3. Now make the attraction stronger at the same temperature. Molecules escape less often: stronger forces mean a higher boiling point and a lower vapour pressure.
  4. Cool it right down. The molecules lock into a regular pattern: a solid.

The four kinds of intermolecular force

All of them are electrostatic: a bit of positive charge attracts a bit of negative charge. Dispersion, dipole–dipole and hydrogen bonding together are also called van der Waals forces. They add up: a molecule feels all the forces that apply to it.

Dispersion

Electrons move around, so at any instant a molecule can have a small, temporary dipole. It induces a dipole in its neighbour and the two attract. Every molecule has dispersion forces. They get stronger with more electrons (larger, more polarisable molecules) and with more contact area.

Dipole–dipole

Polar molecules have a permanent dipole. They line up so that the δ+ end of one faces the δ− end of the next. For molecules of similar mass and size, the more polar one has the stronger attractions.

Hydrogen bond

A special, strong dipole–dipole attraction. An H atom bonded to N, O or F is attracted to a lone pair on an N, O or F atom of another molecule. It explains the high boiling point of water, why ice floats, and how the two strands of DNA stay together.

Ion–dipole

An ion attracts the oppositely charged end of polar molecules, for example Na⁺ and Cl⁻ in water. These forces are strong because the ions carry full charges. They matter most for solutions (topic 9).

    2 · Properties of liquids

    Viscosity and surface tension

    Viscosity is a liquid's resistance to flow. Molecules that attract each other strongly, or long molecules that get tangled, slide past each other less easily. Viscosity drops when the temperature rises, because the molecules have more energy to break free from their neighbours. Water: 1.00 mPa s at 20 °C, 0.47 mPa s at 60 °C.

    Surface tension is the energy needed to increase the surface of a liquid by one unit of area. A molecule in the bulk is pulled equally in all directions. A molecule at the surface has neighbours only below and beside it, so it is pulled inwards. The liquid tries to keep its surface small: drops are round, and a paper clip can lie on water.

    Cohesion and adhesion. Cohesive forces hold a liquid to itself, adhesive forces hold it to a surface. Water attracts glass (adhesion stronger than cohesion), so it creeps up a thin glass tube (capillary action) and its meniscus curves up. Mercury attracts itself much more than glass, so its meniscus curves down.

    Vapour pressure and boiling

    In a closed container, some molecules always have enough kinetic energy to escape from the liquid surface. Others come back. When evaporation and condensation happen at the same rate (a dynamic equilibrium), the pressure of the vapour is the vapour pressure. Weak intermolecular forces mean many molecules escape: the liquid is volatile and has a high vapour pressure.

    A liquid boils when its vapour pressure equals the pressure outside. Then bubbles of vapour can form inside the liquid. The normal boiling point is the boiling point at 1 atm = 101.3 kPa. Lower the outside pressure and the liquid boils at a lower temperature.

    The Clausius–Clapeyron equation: a straight line again

    Vapour pressure rises steeply, almost exponentially, with temperature. Plot \(\ln P\) against \(1/T\) instead (switch the view above) and each liquid gives a nearly straight line:

    \[ \ln P = -\frac{\Delta H_\text{vap}}{RT} + C \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \ln\frac{P_2}{P_1} = \frac{\Delta H_\text{vap}}{R}\left(\frac{1}{T_1} - \frac{1}{T_2}\right) \]

    The slope is \(-\Delta H_\text{vap}/R\). The steeper the line, the more energy it takes to free a molecule from the liquid. \(T\) must be in kelvin, and \(R = 8.314\ \mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}\).

    The equation assumes that \(\Delta H_\text{vap}\) is constant and the vapour is an ideal gas. That is why the slope gives 41 kJ/mol for water near 100 °C, slightly more than the measured 40.7 kJ/mol. It is the same trick as in kinetics (topic 10): turn a curve into a straight line and read a constant off the slope. Arrhenius (topic 11) will look just like this.

    3 · Phase changes: where does the heat go?

    Heat a block of ice at a steady rate and watch the thermometer. The temperature rises, then stops while the ice melts, rises again, and stops again while the water boils. On those flat parts, all the heat goes into pulling molecules apart against their intermolecular forces, not into making them move faster.

    \[ \text{warming one phase: } q = m \, C_s \, \Delta T \qquad\qquad \text{changing phase: } q = n\,\Delta H_\text{fus} \ \text{ or } \ q = n\,\Delta H_\text{vap} \]

    1. With the default settings (1.00 mol, −25 °C to 125 °C), which step takes by far the most heat? Why is boiling so much more expensive than melting? (Think about how far apart the molecules end up.)
    2. Set start 0 °C and end 80 °C. Compare the heat for melting with the heat for warming the water by 80 °C.
    3. Switch the start and end temperature: the same amounts of heat now come out, in reverse order.
    Why do ice cubes cool a drink so well?

    Melting 1 g of ice takes 334 J. That is as much heat as it takes to warm that gram of water from 0 °C to 80 °C, and the ice pulls it all out of your drink while it stays at 0 °C.

    Why do you feel cold when you step out of a pool?

    The water on your skin evaporates. Vaporisation is endothermic (2.4 kJ per gram at skin temperature), and the heat comes from your body. Sweating works the same way.

    Melting, vaporisation and sublimation are endothermic. Freezing, condensation and deposition are exothermic and release the same amounts of heat. Because enthalpy is a state function (week 4): \(\Delta H_\text{sub} = \Delta H_\text{fus} + \Delta H_\text{vap}\).

    4 · Phase diagrams: a map of solid, liquid and gas

    A phase diagram shows which phase is stable at each temperature and pressure. The lines are where two phases coexist in equilibrium:

    • the vapour-pressure curve (liquid ⇌ gas) is the curve from section 2. It ends at the critical point. Beyond it, liquid and gas can no longer be told apart: the substance is a supercritical fluid. Stronger intermolecular forces mean a higher critical temperature.
    • the melting curve (solid ⇌ liquid) is nearly vertical, because pressure barely changes the melting point.
    • the sublimation curve (solid ⇌ gas) is where a solid turns straight into vapour.
    • All three meet at the triple point, the only point where solid, liquid and gas coexist.

    The odd one out: water

    For almost every substance the solid is denser than the liquid, so squeezing a liquid helps it freeze and the melting curve leans to the right (CO₂). Ice is less dense than liquid water: hydrogen bonds lock the molecules into an open hexagonal network that collapses partly when it melts. So squeezing ice helps it melt, and water's melting curve leans to the left.

    The lean is tiny: you need about 130 atm to lower the melting point of ice by just 1 °C. Because ice floats, lakes freeze from the top down, and the ice layer insulates the water beneath it.

    Liquid crystals (Brown 11.7)

    Some substances made of long, rod-shaped molecules do not melt in one step. In between solid and liquid, they form a liquid crystal: the molecules can flow like a liquid but still point in roughly the same direction. In the nematic phase they are only aligned; in smectic phases they are also arranged in layers; in cholesteric phases the direction twists from layer to layer. An electric field can turn the molecules, which changes how they let light through. That is how an LCD screen works.
