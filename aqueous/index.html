Reactions in water: who swaps what

Most of the chemistry around you, from limescale in the kettle to rust on a bike, happens in water. Three kinds of reaction cover most of it, and each one is a transfer: of ions (precipitation), of protons (acid–base) or of electrons (redox). Brown, Chapter 4 (sections 4.1–4.5).

After this week you should be able to

  • calculate a molarity and convert between moles, volume and concentration, including dilution

1 · Solutions and electrolytes

A solution is a homogeneous mixture. The substance present in the largest amount is the solvent; the others, dissolved in it, are solutes. In an aqueous solution (aq) the solvent is water.

  • An electrolyte dissolves by forming ions. An ionic solid such as NaCl dissociates: the lattice falls apart into Na⁺ and Cl⁻, each surrounded by water molecules. The ions can move, so the solution conducts electricity. Polyatomic ions stay in one piece: KNO₃ gives K⁺ and NO₃⁻.
  • A nonelectrolyte dissolves as whole, neutral molecules (sugar, ethanol). No ions, no current.
  • A strong electrolyte is (practically) completely ionized; a weak electrolyte only partly, so most of it stays as molecules. Acetic acid (vinegar) and ammonia are weak electrolytes.

Why is water so good at dissolving ions? Its O atom carries a partial negative charge (δ−) and its H atoms partial positive charges (δ+). Water molecules turn their O towards cations and their H towards anions, and so stabilize the separated ions. (More on why in weeks 7 and 8; whether something dissolves at all is a question of energy and entropy, weeks 4, 5 and 9.)

2 · Precipitation reactions

Mix two solutions of ionic compounds and the cations and anions meet new partners. If one of the new combinations is insoluble, it comes out of solution as a solid, the precipitate:

\[ \mathrm{AgNO_3(aq) + KCl(aq) \longrightarrow AgCl(s) + KNO_3(aq)} \]

The cations and anions trade partners, AX + BY → AY + BX: an exchange or metathesis reaction. Hard water and soap give “soap scum” this way. There are no simple rules from first principles for what is soluble, so chemists use guidelines from experiments (Brown Table 4.1). Two to remember first: all nitrates and acetates are soluble, and all compounds of the alkali metals and of NH₄⁺ are soluble.

Three ways to write the same reaction

The molecular equation writes every compound as a whole formula. But the dissolved compounds really exist as separate ions, so the complete ionic equation writes them as ions. Ions that appear unchanged on both sides take no part: they are spectator ions. Cancel them and you get the net ionic equation, which shows what actually happens.

3 · Acids, bases and neutralization

An acid is a substance that ionizes in water to form H⁺ ions: it is a proton donor (an H⁺ is just a proton). A base accepts H⁺ ions. Many bases do this by releasing OH⁻, which then reacts with H⁺; but a base does not need to contain OH⁻. Ammonia takes an H⁺ from water:

\[ \mathrm{NH_3(aq) + H_2O(l) \rightleftharpoons NH_4^+(aq) + OH^-(aq)} \]

Acids with one ionizable H are monoprotic (HCl, HNO₃), with two diprotic (H₂SO₄):

\(\mathrm{H_2SO_4(aq) \to H^+(aq) + HSO_4^-(aq)}\)
\(\mathrm{HSO_4^-(aq) \rightleftharpoons H^+(aq) + SO_4^{2-}(aq)}\)

The first step is complete, the second only partial. Not every H counts: the H atoms of CH₃COOH bonded to C never come off; only the one on O does.

Strong or weak tells you how far an acid ionizes, not how dangerous it is. HF is a weak acid, yet very reactive and corrosive because of the F⁻ ion: it attacks glass and bone. Acid strength is put on a number scale (pH) in week 13.

Neutralization

An acid and a base react to give water and a salt, an ionic compound whose cation comes from the base and whose anion comes from the acid. With a strong acid and a strong base, everything except the water is a spectator:

\[ \mathrm{HCl(aq) + NaOH(aq) \to H_2O(l) + NaCl(aq)} \qquad\text{net:}\qquad \mathrm{H^+(aq) + OH^-(aq) \to H_2O(l)} \]

Acid on limescale

Hard water leaves limescale (Kalk) in kettles and showers: a precipitate of calcium carbonate, \(\mathrm{Ca^{2+}(aq) + CO_3^{2-}(aq) \to CaCO_3(s)}\). The carbonate is a base, so an acid such as vinegar (acetic acid) dissolves it, and the bubbles are CO₂:

\[ \mathrm{CaCO_3(s) + 2\,CH_3COOH(aq) \to Ca(CH_3COO)_2(aq) + H_2O(l) + CO_2(g)} \]

The H₂CO₃ that would form falls apart into H₂O and CO₂. Acetic acid is weak, so it is written as molecules even in the ionic equation.

4 · Oxidation–reduction reactions

In a redox reaction electrons move from one atom to another. Rust is the classic example: iron gives electrons to O₂ in the air and becomes Fe₂O₃. Silver tarnishes the same way with H₂S, to Ag₂S.

  • Oxidation: an atom, ion or molecule loses electrons and becomes more positive. (The name comes from the first examples studied: metals reacting with oxygen.)
  • Reduction: it gains electrons, so its charge is “reduced”.
  • The two always go together: electrons given up by one species must be taken by another. LEO the lion says GER: Losing Electrons is Oxidation, Gaining Electrons is Reduction.
\[ 2\,\mathrm{Ca(s) + O_2(g) \to 2\,CaO(s)} \qquad \mathrm{Ca \to Ca^{2+}}\ \text{(oxidized)},\quad \mathrm{O \to O^{2-}}\ \text{(reduced)} \]

To keep track of electrons in molecules too, every atom gets an oxidation number (oxidation state): the charge it would have if all its bonds were ionic. Apply the rules in this order:

  1. An atom in an element (Fe, O₂, Cl₂) has oxidation number 0.
  2. A monatomic ion has its charge (Na⁺ +1, S²⁻ −2). In compounds, group 1 metals are always +1, group 2 metals +2, Al +3.
  3. Nonmetals are usually negative:
    • F is always −1.
    • O is −2, except in peroxides (O₂²⁻, H₂O₂): −1.
    • H is +1 with nonmetals, −1 with metals (NaH).
    • Cl, Br, I are −1, except when combined with O (then positive).
  4. The oxidation numbers add up to the overall charge: 0 for a compound, the ion's charge for a polyatomic ion.

What is the oxidation number of the highlighted element?

    In a redox reaction, the oxidation number of the oxidized element goes up and that of the reduced element goes down. The species that gets oxidized is the reductant (reducing agent): it reduces the other one. The species that gets reduced is the oxidant:

    \[ \overset{0}{\mathrm{Mg}}\mathrm{(s)} + 2\,\overset{+1}{\mathrm{H}}\overset{-1}{\mathrm{Cl}}\mathrm{(aq)} \longrightarrow \overset{+2}{\mathrm{Mg}}\overset{-1}{\mathrm{Cl}}{}_2\mathrm{(aq)} + \overset{0}{\mathrm{H}}{}_2\mathrm{(g)} \]

    Mg goes from 0 to +2: oxidized, the reductant. H goes from +1 to 0: reduced, HCl is the oxidant. Cl stays −1: a spectator. A metal reacting with an acid or a metal salt like this is a displacement reaction, A + BX → AX + B.

    5 · The activity series

    Which metals are oxidized easily? The activity series (Brown Table 4.5) lists metals from the easiest to oxidize (top) to the hardest (bottom). Two rules follow:

    • A metal is oxidized by the ions of any metal below it.
    • Only metals above hydrogen react with acids (H⁺) to give H₂ gas.

    The noble metals at the bottom (Ag, Pt, Au) hardly react, which is why gold stays shiny and has served as money for thousands of years. Lithium at the top gives up its electron most easily: no surprise that it ends up in batteries (week 14).

    6 · Concentration: molarity

    To calculate with solutions we need to say how much solute is in them. The most common measure:

    \[ \text{molarity } M = \frac{\text{moles of solute}}{\text{volume of solution in litres}} \qquad 1\ \mathrm{M} = 1\ \mathrm{mol/L} \]

    Note: litres of solution, not of water. To make 1.00 L of a 1.00 M solution, dissolve 1.00 mol of solute and then fill up to the 1 L mark. If you know two of \(M\), \(V\) and \(n\), you get the third: \(n = M \times V\). Molarity is not the only way to give a concentration (week 9 has more).

    Dilution

    Add water to a solution and the amount of solute does not change, only the volume. So

    \[ n = M_1 V_1 = M_2 V_2 \]
