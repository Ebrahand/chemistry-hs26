Chemical kinetics II: temperature, mechanisms and catalysts

Last week ended with a rate law, rate = k[A]m[B]n, and three open questions. Why do we heat reactions? Why is one spontaneous reaction fast and another one slow? And what do the orders m and n actually mean? This week answers all three. Brown, Chapter 14 (sections 14.5–14.7).

After this week you should be able to
  • explain with the collision model why molecules must collide with enough energy and the right orientation
  • draw an energy profile and label the activation energy \(E_a\), the transition state and \(\Delta E\); get \(E_a\) of the reverse reaction
  • use the Arrhenius equation \(k = A\,e^{-E_a/RT}\), and find \(E_a\) from \(\ln k\) against \(1/T\) or from two rate constants
  • write the rate law of an elementary reaction from its molecularity, and the rate law of a mechanism with a slow first step
  • say what a catalyst changes (the path, \(E_a\)) and what it does not change (\(\Delta E\), the overall reaction)

1 · The collision model: why heat speeds things up

Before two molecules can react, they have to meet. So the reaction rate is proportional to the number of collisions per second. More molecules per litre means more collisions: that is where the concentrations in the rate law come from. Higher temperature means faster molecules (kinetic-molecular theory, week 2), so they collide more often and harder.

But only a tiny fraction of collisions actually leads to a reaction. In a mixture of H₂ and I₂ at room temperature, each molecule collides about 10¹⁰ times per second, yet only about one collision in 10¹³ forms HI (Brown 14.5). A collision only works if two things are right:

1. Enough energy

The molecules must hit hard enough to stretch and break bonds: their collision energy must be at least the activation energy \(E_a\). Gentle collisions just bounce.

2. The right orientation

The atoms that are to form the new bond must actually touch. For \(\mathrm{A} + \mathrm{BC} \to \mathrm{AB} + \mathrm{C}\), A has to hit the B end of BC. Hitting the C end does nothing, however hard.

Successful collisions only flash; the molecules are kept, so that the counts stay steady.

2 · Activation energy: the hill between reactants and products

Reactants are stable, and so are products. In between, the bonds are half broken and half formed. That arrangement is less stable, so it has a higher energy. The arrangement at the top of the hill is the transition state (or activated complex, marked ‡). It lasts only as long as a molecular vibration and can never be isolated. The activation energy \(E_a\) is the energy needed to get from the reactants up to it. Think of tipping over a bottle: you first have to lift its centre of mass over the tipping point.

For the simplest case, an atom A meeting a molecule BC in a straight line, the energy depends on just two numbers: the A–B distance and the B–C distance. Plot the energy as a height above those two distances and you get a landscape, the potential-energy surface. The reactants sit in one valley (B–C bonded, A far away), the products in another (A–B bonded, C far away). The easiest way from one to the other leads over a mountain pass: the transition state. Going over the pass takes less energy than taking BC apart completely first (the high flat plateau), which is why reactions go through a transition state at all.

The energy profile in Brown (section 14.5) is this landscape seen along the path drawn on it: the height of the path, unrolled into a flat graph of energy against reaction progress. That 2D profile is what you draw in exercises and in the exam. Two things to take from it:

  • The rate depends on \(E_a\), not on \(\Delta E\). A larger \(E_a\) means a bigger hill and a slower reaction. \(\Delta E\) decides whether the reaction gives out or takes in heat, not how fast it goes. That answers last week's question: two spontaneous reactions can have very different speeds because their \(E_a\) can be large or small.
  • The reverse reaction climbs the same hill from the other side: \(E_a(\text{reverse}) = E_a(\text{forward}) - \Delta E\). For an exothermic reaction (\(\Delta E < 0\)) the reverse reaction has the bigger hill and is slower. You will need this in week 12.

Why only a few molecules make it over

Molecules carry energy into a collision through their motion, and their kinetic energies are spread out over a wide range (the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution, week 2). Only the molecules in the tail with \(E \ge E_a\) can react. The fraction of them is

\[ f = e^{-E_a/RT} \qquad (R = 8.314\ \mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}},\ T \text{ in kelvin}) \]

The sphere marks all velocities with \(\tfrac12 M u^2 = E_a\), so the molecules outside it have \(E \ge E_a\). 3 · The Arrhenius equation

Put the pieces of the collision model together. The rate constant is the number of collisions per second, times the fraction that are properly oriented, times the fraction with enough energy. The first two change only slowly with temperature; Arrhenius lumped them into one constant, the frequency factor \(A\) (not to be confused with a reactant called A). The third is the exponential:

\[ k = A\, e^{-E_a/RT} \]

Take the natural log of both sides and you get a straight line:

\[ \ln k = -\frac{E_a}{R}\left(\frac{1}{T}\right) + \ln A \qquad \longleftrightarrow \qquad y = m\,x + b \]

Plot \(\ln k\) against \(1/T\): the slope is \(-E_a/R\) and the intercept is \(\ln A\). If you only have two rate constants, subtract the two equations and \(A\) cancels:

\[ \ln\frac{k_1}{k_2} = \frac{E_a}{R}\left(\frac{1}{T_2} - \frac{1}{T_1}\right) \]

Both forms assume that \(A\) and \(E_a\) do not change over the temperature range. \(E_a\) never changes when you heat: heating changes how many molecules can get over the hill, not the height of the hill.

The +10 °C rule of thumb, and ants

Around room temperature many reactions run about twice as fast for every 10 °C. With the two-temperature form you can see which activation energy that corresponds to: \(k_{308}/k_{298} = 2\) gives \(E_a = R\ln 2 \,/\, (1/298 - 1/308) \approx 53\) kJ/mol, a typical value. The lecture's example: ants are cold-blooded, and a century ago Shapley timed them running at different temperatures (H. Shapley, PNAS 6, 204, 1920). Their speed roughly doubled with every +10 °C, as if the ant were one big chemical reaction with \(E_a\) of about 50 kJ/mol. Move the \(E_a\) slider above and read the "+10 °C" row.

4 · Reaction mechanisms: what do the orders mean?

A balanced equation tells you what goes in and what comes out, not how it happens. Most reactions run through a sequence of simple steps, the reaction mechanism. Each step is an elementary reaction: one event in which typically one bond breaks or forms. The number of molecules that collide in an elementary reaction is its molecularity.

For an elementary reaction, and only for one, you can write the rate law straight from the equation. Twice as many A molecules give twice as many collisions, so the exponents are the coefficients:

MolecularityElementary reactionRate law
UnimolecularA → products\(k[\mathrm{A}]\)common
BimolecularA + A → products\(k[\mathrm{A}]^2\)common
BimolecularA + B → products\(k[\mathrm{A}][\mathrm{B}]\)
TermolecularA + A + A → products\(k[\mathrm{A}]^3\)rare: three molecules must meet at once
TermolecularA + A + B → products\(k[\mathrm{A}]^2[\mathrm{B}]\)
TermolecularA + B + C → products\(k[\mathrm{A}][\mathrm{B}][\mathrm{C}]\)

That is the whole list (Brown Table 14.3). A collision of four molecules practically never happens. The catch: from a balanced equation alone you cannot tell whether it is elementary. Last week's statement "the orders m, n are not necessarily the coefficients" comes from exactly this.

Multistep reactions and intermediates

The lecture's example, ozone falling apart under UV-B light:

\[ \begin{array}{lrcl} \text{Step 1:} & \mathrm{O_3(g)} & \longrightarrow & \mathrm{O_2(g)} + \mathrm{O(g)} \\ \text{Step 2:} & \mathrm{O_3(g)} + \mathrm{O(g)} & \longrightarrow & 2\,\mathrm{O_2(g)} \\ \hline \text{Sum:} & 2\,\mathrm{O_3(g)} & \longrightarrow & 3\,\mathrm{O_2(g)} \end{array} \]

The steps must add up to the overall reaction. O(g) is formed in step 1 and used up in step 2, so it cancels: it is an intermediate. Unlike a transition state, an intermediate sits in a dip of the energy profile; it can live long enough to be detected, sometimes even isolated.

The rate-determining step

Each step has its own rate constant. Often one step is much slower than all the others, and the reaction cannot go faster than that step: it is the rate-determining (rate-limiting) step. Brown's picture is a road with two toll plazas: if the first one is jammed, opening more booths at the second one does not get cars home any sooner. Try it with a two-step reaction A → I → P:

A slow first step: the rate law of the mechanism

When the first step is rate-determining, the overall rate law is simply the rate law of step 1. Brown's example: below 225 °C, \(\mathrm{NO_2(g)} + \mathrm{CO(g)} \to \mathrm{NO(g)} + \mathrm{CO_2(g)}\) has the measured rate law rate = \(k[\mathrm{NO_2}]^2\), with CO not in it at all (last week's table). A mechanism that fits:

\[ \begin{array}{lrcll} \text{Step 1:} & \mathrm{NO_2} + \mathrm{NO_2} & \xrightarrow{k_1} & \mathrm{NO_3} + \mathrm{NO} & \text{(slow)} \\ \text{Step 2:} & \mathrm{NO_3} + \mathrm{CO} & \xrightarrow{k_2} & \mathrm{NO_2} + \mathrm{CO_2} & \text{(fast)} \end{array} \qquad \text{rate} = k_1[\mathrm{NO_2}]^2 \]

CO only reacts after the bottleneck, so its concentration does not matter. This is how mechanisms are found:

  1. measure the rate law in the lab;
  2. propose a mechanism of elementary steps that add up to the overall reaction;
  3. derive its rate law and compare. If it disagrees, the mechanism is wrong. If it agrees, the mechanism is consistent with the data, which is not a proof: another mechanism might fit just as well.

If a later step is the slow one, the derivation is harder: you usually assume that the fast steps before it are at equilibrium. That needs week 12. Brown 14.6 has worked examples.

5 · Catalysis

A catalyst speeds up a reaction without being produced or consumed in the overall reaction. It may take part in a step, but a later step gives it back. Since \(k = A\,e^{-E_a/RT}\), a catalyst can only work through \(A\) or \(E_a\): it can hold the molecules in a better orientation (larger \(A\)), or, more often, it opens a different path, a new mechanism, with a lower activation energy. Lowering \(E_a\) is usually the bigger effect, because \(E_a\) sits in the exponent.

The catalysed route has two steps and an intermediate in the dip between them; its higher pass (the slow step) sets \(E_a\). The valleys at both ends are shared, so \(\Delta E\) of the reaction is the same on both routes.

Types of catalysts

Homogeneous

Catalyst in the same phase as the reactants. Acids (H⁺) catalyse many reactions in solution. In the stratosphere, Cl atoms from old CFCs catalyse ozone destruction: Cl + O₃ → ClO + O₂, then ClO + O → Cl + O₂. Cl comes back after every cycle, ready to destroy the next ozone molecule.

Heterogeneous

Catalyst in a different phase, usually a solid with gases flowing over it. The molecules stick to the surface, where bonds are weakened. A car's catalytic converter uses Pt, Pd and Rh to turn NOx into N₂ and O₂; the Haber–Bosch process uses iron.

Enzymes

Nature's catalysts: large proteins that hold the reactants in exactly the right position. They often speed reactions up by factors of millions or more, at body temperature.

Feeding the world: nitrogen fixation

Plants need nitrogen, and around 1900 the natural fertilisers (bird guano from Peru, saltpetre NaNO₃ from Chile) were running out. Making ammonia from the air, \(\mathrm{N_2} + 3\,\mathrm{H_2} \rightleftharpoons 2\,\mathrm{NH_3}\), is spontaneous at room temperature (\(\Delta G^\circ = -33\) kJ, week 5) but extremely slow: the N≡N triple bond gives a huge activation energy. Haber found a catalyst (osmium, 1909) and Bosch scaled the process up at BASF with cheaper iron catalysts (1910–1914).
