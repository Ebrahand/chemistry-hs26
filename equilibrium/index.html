Chemical equilibrium

So far we treated reactions as running one way, from reactants to products. In a closed container they run both ways at once, and they stop changing when the two directions balance. Where that balance lies decides how much product you can get at most. Brown, Chapter 15 (sections 15.1–15.5) and section 19.7.

After this week you should be able to
  • explain dynamic equilibrium as equal forward and reverse rates, not as equal amounts
  • write \(K_c\) and \(K_p\) for any balanced equation (homogeneous or heterogeneous) and convert with \(K_p = K_c(RT)^{\Delta n}\)
  • change \(K\) when the equation is reversed, multiplied, or added to another one
  • compare \(Q\) with \(K\) to predict the direction of a reaction, and calculate equilibrium amounts with a table
  • predict with Le Châtelier's principle what a change in concentration, pressure, temperature or a catalyst does
  • relate \(K\) to \(\Delta G^\circ\): \(\Delta G^\circ = -RT\ln K\)

1 · Dynamic equilibrium: both directions at once

You have met dynamic equilibrium before: in a closed bottle, water molecules leave the liquid and come back at the same rate (vapour pressure, week 8); in a saturated solution, ions dissolve and crystallise at the same rate (week 9). Something still happens all the time, but the two processes balance, so nothing changes on the whole. A chemical reaction does the same. Take \(\mathrm{A} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{B}\), with both directions elementary steps (week 11):

\[ \text{rate}_f = k_f[\mathrm{A}] \qquad \text{rate}_r = k_r[\mathrm{B}] \]

At equilibrium the rates are equal, \(k_f[\mathrm{A}] = k_r[\mathrm{B}]\), so

\[ \frac{[\mathrm{B}]}{[\mathrm{A}]} = \frac{k_f}{k_r} = \text{constant} \equiv K \]
  1. Start with all A, then with all B. The same ratio B : A comes out both times: the equilibrium does not care where you start.
  2. Switch to "Rates". At first the forward rate is large and the reverse rate zero; they meet, and then stay equal. That, not equal amounts of A and B, is equilibrium.
  3. Tick "Catalyst": both rate constants grow by the same factor. Equilibrium comes faster, but the ratio does not change.
  4. At equilibrium, add A or take away B. The system reacts and goes back to the same ratio (module 4).

Each molecule turns over at random with probability \(k\,\Delta t\) per time step, so the counts flicker around equilibrium, as real molecules do. With 10²³ molecules instead of 200, the flicker is far too small to measure.

Lessons (Brown 15.1): at equilibrium the concentrations no longer change, but both reactions keep running at equal rates. Equilibrium needs a closed system: nothing may escape. And at a given temperature, the same equilibrium is reached from either side.

2 · The equilibrium constant

In 1864 Guldberg and Waage found by experiment the law of mass action: for any reaction

\[ a\,\mathrm{A} + b\,\mathrm{B} \rightleftharpoons d\,\mathrm{D} + e\,\mathrm{E} \qquad K_c = \frac{[\mathrm{D}]^d\,[\mathrm{E}]^e}{[\mathrm{A}]^a\,[\mathrm{B}]^b} \quad\leftarrow\ \text{products over reactants} \]

with the equilibrium concentrations in mol/L. Brown's evidence: four sealed tubes of N₂O₄ and NO₂ at 100 °C, started very differently, end with different concentrations but the same \(K_c\):

Rate laws need a mechanism, K does not

In a rate law the exponents come from experiment (week 10) unless the reaction is elementary. In \(K\) the exponents are always the coefficients of the balanced equation, whatever the mechanism. Close to equilibrium, every reaction behaves as if it were elementary, and thermodynamics proves the law of mass action in general (module 5).

Kp, units, and what the size of K tells you

For gases you can use partial pressures instead: \(K_p = \dfrac{P_\mathrm{D}^d\,P_\mathrm{E}^e}{P_\mathrm{A}^a\,P_\mathrm{B}^b}\). With \(P = [\,]\,RT\) from the ideal-gas law:

\[ K_p = K_c\,(RT)^{\Delta n} \qquad \Delta n = (\text{moles of gaseous products}) - (\text{moles of gaseous reactants}) \]

with pressures in bar, concentrations in mol/L and \(R = 0.08314\ \mathrm{L\,bar\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}\). In general \(K_c \ne K_p\); they are equal only when \(\Delta n = 0\).

K has no units. Strictly, \(K\) is built from activities: each concentration divided by 1 M, each pressure divided by 1 bar. The units cancel, and for an ideal mixture the numbers do not change. Pure solids and pure liquids have activity 1.

\(K \gg 1\): the equilibrium lies to the right; products dominate.
\(K \ll 1\): the equilibrium lies to the left; reactants dominate.

Not "reactants = products at equilibrium". What is equal is the two rates.

Heterogeneous equilibria

When the substances are in different phases, pure solids and pure liquids are left out of \(K\) (activity 1): their concentration is fixed by their density, however much of them there is. For lime burning, \(\mathrm{CaCO_3(s)} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{CaO(s)} + \mathrm{CO_2(g)}\), simply \(K_p = P_\mathrm{CO_2}\): at a given temperature the CO₂ pressure over the solids is fixed, as long as some of both solids is there. For a slightly soluble salt in a saturated solution, e.g. \(\mathrm{PbCl_2(s)} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{Pb^{2+}(aq)} + 2\,\mathrm{Cl^-(aq)}\), \(K_c = [\mathrm{Pb^{2+}}][\mathrm{Cl^-}]^2\) has a special name, the solubility product \(K_{sp}\).

3 · The reaction quotient Q, and calculating equilibria

Put concentrations (or pressures) that are not at equilibrium into the same expression and you get the reaction quotient \(Q\). Comparing it with \(K\) tells you which way the reaction will run:

\(Q < K\)too little product: the reaction runs forward (→) and \(Q\) grows
\(Q = K\)at equilibrium: no net change
\(Q > K\)too much product: the reaction runs in reverse (←) and \(Q\) falls

4 · Le Châtelier's principle and the Haber process

If a system at equilibrium is disturbed by a change in temperature, pressure or concentration, the system shifts its equilibrium to counteract the disturbance. (Henri Le Châtelier, 1884)

Concentration

Add a substance and the reaction consumes some of it; remove one and the reaction makes more of it. \(K\) stays the same: only \(Q\) was moved away from it. Industry removes NH₃ as it forms (by liquefying it) to pull the Haber reaction forward.

Pressure (volume)

Squeeze a gas mixture and it shifts to the side with fewer moles of gas; expand it and it shifts to the side with more. No change if \(\Delta n = 0\). Adding an inert gas such as argon at constant volume changes no partial pressure: no shift.

Temperature

Treat heat as a reactant (endothermic) or a product (exothermic). Heating an exothermic reaction shifts it left, heating an endothermic one shifts it right. This is the only change that alters \(K\) itself.

Catalyst

Lowers the hill for both directions equally, so \(k_f\) and \(k_r\) grow by the same factor and \(K = k_f/k_r\) is unchanged. Equilibrium is reached faster, nothing more.

Why temperature changes K: the activation energies

\(K = k_f/k_r\), and both rate constants follow Arrhenius. For an exothermic reaction the reverse direction has the bigger hill, \(E_a(\text{rev}) = E_a(\text{fwd}) - \Delta E > E_a(\text{fwd})\) (week 11). A larger \(E_a\) means a stronger response to temperature, so heating speeds up the reverse reaction more than the forward one, and \(K\) falls. Example: \(E_a(\text{fwd}) = 50\) kJ/mol, \(\Delta E = -40\) kJ/mol, so \(E_a(\text{rev}) = 90\) kJ/mol. From 25 °C to 35 °C, \(k_f\) grows by a factor 1.93 but \(k_r\) by 3.25, so \(K\) drops to 0.59 of its value.

Why Haber and Bosch needed 300 bar

Last week's story continues. \(\mathrm{N_2(g)} + 3\,\mathrm{H_2(g)} \rightleftharpoons 2\,\mathrm{NH_3(g)}\) is exothermic (\(\Delta H^\circ = -92\) kJ) and has \(\Delta n = -2\). It is far too slow at room temperature, so it is run hot, at 400–500 °C, even with the iron catalyst. But heat pushes this exothermic equilibrium back towards N₂ and H₂. The fix is pressure: four moles of gas become two, so squeezing the mixture pushes it back towards NH₃.

5 · Free energy and the equilibrium constant

Week 5 gave the criterion for spontaneity at constant \(T\) and \(P\): \(\Delta G < 0\). But which \(\Delta G\)? \(\Delta G^\circ\) belongs to standard conditions (every gas at 1 bar, every solute at 1 M). For any other mixture, with its reaction quotient \(Q\):

\[ \Delta G = \Delta G^\circ + RT\ln Q \qquad (R = 8.314\ \mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}) \]

A reaction runs as long as \(\Delta G < 0\). It stops, at equilibrium, when \(\Delta G = 0\) and \(Q = K\):

\[ 0 = \Delta G^\circ + RT\ln K \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \Delta G^\circ = -RT\ln K \qquad K = e^{-\Delta G^\circ/RT} \]

\(\Delta G^\circ < 0\) gives \(K > 1\) (products favoured); \(\Delta G^\circ > 0\) gives \(K < 1\). Because \(\Delta G^\circ\) sits in an exponent, a few kJ make a big difference: at 25 °C, every 5.7 kJ/mol is a factor of 10 in \(K\).

Why a reaction stops in the middle: the bottom of the free-energy valley

Take a gas reaction \(\mathrm{A(g)} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{B(g)}\) at a total of 1 bar, and follow \(G\) of the mixture as A turns into B. Without mixing, \(G\) would be a straight line from pure A to pure B, and the reaction would run all the way to the lower end. But a mixture has more microstates than either pure gas (week 5), and that mixing entropy bends the line into a valley. Equilibrium is its lowest point; \(\Delta G\) is the slope at the present composition.
