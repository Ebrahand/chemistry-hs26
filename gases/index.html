Gases: billions of tiny billiard balls

Helium and sulfur hexafluoride are chemically nothing alike, yet as gases they obey the same law, \(PV = nRT\). Why? Because a gas is mostly empty space with molecules flying through it, and then only the number of molecules matters. Brown, Chapter 10.

1 · What gases have in common, and pressure

Different gases have very different chemistry, but very similar physical properties:

  • they expand to fill their container, and they can be compressed;
  • they always mix completely (form homogeneous mixtures);
  • the molecules take up only a tiny fraction of the volume: in air at 1 atm, about 0.1 %.

To describe a gas you need only four variables: pressure \(P\), volume \(V\), temperature \(T\) and amount \(n\) (moles). \(V\) and \(n\) are extensive (they grow with the size of the sample), \(P\) and \(T\) are intensive (they do not).

Pressure is force per area: \(P = F/A\), in pascal, \(1\ \mathrm{Pa} = 1\ \mathrm{N/m^2}\). In a gas the force comes from molecules hitting the walls, billions of times per second. The atmosphere presses on you because the air above you has weight: a column of air 1 m² across, up to the top of the atmosphere, has a mass of about 10 000 kg, so \(F = mg \approx 1\times10^5\ \mathrm{N}\) and \(P \approx 100\ \mathrm{kPa}\).

Torricelli's barometer: fill a tube with mercury and turn it upside down in a dish. The column falls until its weight per area equals the pressure of the atmosphere on the dish. For a column of height \(h\) and density \(\rho\), the hydrostatic pressure is

\[ P = \frac{F}{A} = \frac{m g}{A} = \frac{\rho\,(A h)\,g}{A} = \rho\, g\, h \]

2 · The gas laws: change one thing, watch another

Robert Boyle was the first to change one variable on purpose, keep the others fixed, and measure how another one responded. That idea is how science and engineering find laws. For gases it gives three:

LawHeld constantRelationshipWhy, molecule by molecule
Boyle\(n, T\)\(PV = \text{const}\), \(P \propto 1/V\)half the volume: each molecule hits the walls twice as often
Charles\(n, P\)\(V/T = \text{const}\), \(V \propto T\)hotter molecules hit harder; the piston moves out until the pressure is back
Avogadro\(P, T\)\(V/n = \text{const}\), \(V \propto n\)more molecules need more room for the same pressure

Each dot stands for many molecules: the box shows 40 dots per mole. Speeds follow the temperature (\(u \propto \sqrt{T}\)); the numbers come from \(PV = nRT\).

Charles's law is only a straight line through the origin in kelvin. In °C the lines for all gases point to the same temperature, −273.15 °C, where the volume would be zero. That is absolute zero, 0 K. A change of 1 °C is the same as a change of 1 K. Avogadro's hypothesis: equal volumes of different gases at the same \(T\) and \(P\) contain the same number of molecules, even though the molecules have very different masses.

3 · The ideal gas law

Put the three laws together: \(V \propto \dfrac{nT}{P}\). Call the constant of proportionality \(R\):

\[ PV = nRT \]

The gas constant \(R\) is the same number in different units:

8.314J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ = m³ Pa mol⁻¹ K⁻¹
8.314L kPa mol⁻¹ K⁻¹
0.08206L atm mol⁻¹ K⁻¹
62.36L torr mol⁻¹ K⁻¹
An ideal gas is a model: the molecules take up no volume and do not attract or repel each other. Then the gas only cares about how many molecules there are, not which. Real gases are close to ideal at low pressure and high temperature (section 6). STP (standard temperature and pressure): 0 °C = 273.15 K and 1 atm. One mole of any ideal gas at STP fills 22.41 L, the molar volume.

The number of moles counts gas particles: a reaction A(g) → B(g) + C(g) in a closed, rigid flask doubles the pressure at constant \(T\).

Worked example from Lecture 2: the rising balloon (type 3)

A balloon of 6.0 L at 101.3 kPa and 22 °C rises until the pressure is 45.6 kPa and the temperature −21 °C. The amount of gas is fixed, so

\[ \frac{P_1V_1}{T_1} = \frac{P_2V_2}{T_2} \quad\Rightarrow\quad V_2 = V_1\,\frac{P_1}{P_2}\,\frac{T_2}{T_1} = 6.0\ \mathrm{L}\times\frac{101.3}{45.6}\times\frac{252\ \mathrm{K}}{295\ \mathrm{K}} = 11\ \mathrm{L} \]

The lower pressure more than doubles the volume; the cold shrinks it a bit. Temperatures in kelvin, always: in °C the ratio would be −21/22, a negative volume.

Density and molar mass: which balloon floats?

Since \(n/V = P/RT\) and \(n = m/M_w\), the density of a gas is

\[ \rho = \frac{m}{V} = \frac{P\,M_w}{RT} \qquad\text{and so}\qquad M_w = \frac{\rho RT}{P} \]

At the same \(P\) and \(T\), a gas's density is proportional to its molar mass. A balloon floats if its gas is less dense than the air around it (\(M_w\) of air ≈ 28.97 g/mol). Hot air works because density also drops with \(T\).

4 · Mixtures and partial pressures

In an ideal gas the molecules ignore each other, so in a mixture each gas behaves as if it were alone. The partial pressure \(P_i\) of gas \(i\) is the pressure it would exert alone in the same volume, and the total is the sum (Dalton's law):

\[ P_\text{tot} = P_1 + P_2 + \cdots = (n_1 + n_2 + \cdots)\frac{RT}{V} = n_\text{tot}\frac{RT}{V} \]

Dividing one by the other: \(P_i = X_i\,P_\text{tot}\), where \(X_i = n_i / n_\text{tot}\) is the mole fraction, a number between 0 and 1 without units.

5 · Kinetic-molecular theory: why the gas law works

The ideal gas law describes what happens; the kinetic-molecular theory explains why. Its model of a gas:

  1. Gas molecules are in constant, random motion.
  2. Their own volume is negligible compared with the volume of the container.
  3. They collide with each other and the walls, but do not attract or repel each other.
  4. Energy is passed on in collisions, but the average kinetic energy of the molecules stays constant at a given temperature and is proportional to the absolute temperature.

Pressure comes from molecules hitting the walls: how often and how hard. Double the absolute temperature and the average kinetic energy \(\bar{E}_k = \tfrac12 m\overline{u^2}\) doubles, and so does the pressure, at constant volume. This is the molecular meaning of temperature: \(T\) measures the average kinetic energy of the molecules, and it is the same for every kind of molecule at the same \(T\). So lighter molecules must move faster.

Speed distributions

Not all molecules move at the same speed: collisions constantly speed some up and slow others down. The distribution has three useful “typical” speeds:

  • \(u_\text{mp}\), the most probable speed (the peak);
  • \(u_\text{av}\), the average speed;
  • \(u_\text{rms}\), the speed of a molecule with the average kinetic energy: \(\displaystyle u_\text{rms} = \sqrt{\frac{3RT}{M_w}}\), with \(M_w\) in kg/mol.
AB

Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution for an ideal gas: \(f(u) = 4\pi \left(\frac{M}{2\pi RT}\right)^{3/2} u^2 e^{-Mu^2/2RT}\) (not needed for the exam). It gives \(u_\text{mp} = \sqrt{2RT/M}\), \(u_\text{av} = \sqrt{8RT/\pi M}\), \(u_\text{rms} = \sqrt{3RT/M}\), always in the order \(u_\text{mp} < u_\text{av} < u_\text{rms}\). \(u_\text{rms}\) lies right of the peak because the kinetic energy goes with \(u^2\), so the fast molecules count extra. As \(T\) rises, a bigger fraction of molecules is fast: remember this for chemical kinetics (week 10).

Diffusion, mean free path and effusion

At room temperature the molecules of air move at about 500 m/s, yet a smell takes seconds to cross a room. The reason: collisions. At 1 atm a molecule hits another about 10¹⁰ times per second, so it travels only a tiny distance, the mean free path, before its direction is randomized. Diffusion, the spreading of one gas through another, is a random walk.

\(\lambda = k_BT / (\sqrt2\,\pi d^2 P)\) for air (d ≈ 0.37 nm) at 25 °C; the 100 km value uses about 0.03 Pa. At 1 atm λ ≈ 60–70 nm; at 100 km altitude about 10 cm.

Effusion is the escape of a gas through a tiny hole into a vacuum (Brown 10.7). The rate is proportional to the speed of the molecules, so lighter gases escape faster (Graham's law):

\[ \frac{r_1}{r_2} = \sqrt{\frac{M_2}{M_1}} \]

The same √M dependence is one reason a helium party balloon goes flat overnight while an air-filled one lasts for days: He atoms (4 g/mol) are √(29/4) ≈ 2.7 times faster than the molecules of air. Uranium was enriched this way: ²³⁵UF₆ effuses 0.4 % faster than ²³⁸UF₆.

6 · Real gases

The ideal gas law is incredibly useful, but real gases are not ideal when the molecules are close together. A handy measure is the compressibility factor \(Z = PV/nRT\), which is exactly 1 for an ideal gas. Two things go wrong:

  • Attraction (at moderate pressure and low temperature): molecules start to “stick” to each other. A molecule about to hit the wall is pulled back by its neighbours and hits it less hard. The pressure is lower than ideal: \(Z < 1\).
  • Molecular volume (at high pressure): the molecules themselves fill part of the container, so the space they can move in is smaller than \(V\). \(Z > 1\).

The van der Waals equation keeps the form of \(PV = nRT\) but corrects both:

\[ \left(P + \frac{n^2 a}{V^2}\right)\left(V - nb\right) = nRT \]

\(a\) measures how strongly the molecules attract each other, \(b\) how much volume a mole of them occupies. Both tend to grow with the size of the molecule.
