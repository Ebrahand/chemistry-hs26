Thermodynamics I: energy, heat and enthalpy

1 · Energy is conserved: the first law

Thermodynamics is the study of energy and how it changes form. Thermochemistry is the part of it that asks how much heat a chemical reaction gives off or takes up. Energy comes in two kinds: kinetic energy (motion) and potential energy (stored, for example in chemical bonds).

First, draw a line around what you are interested in. That is the system, usually the reactants and products. Everything else is the surroundings.

The internal energy \(E\) of a system is all of its kinetic and potential energy. We cannot measure \(E\) itself, only how it changes: \(\Delta E = E_\text{final} - E_\text{initial}\). The first law of thermodynamics says energy is neither created nor destroyed. So if the system's energy changes, the energy must have crossed the boundary, as heat or as work:

\[ \Delta E = q + w \]

Brown (and this course) look at everything from the system's point of view. Think of the system as a bank vault: whatever goes in counts positive, whatever comes out counts negative.

Careful: many physics and engineering books write \(\Delta E = q - w\) with \(w\) the work done by the system. Same physics, different sign. In this course we use Brown's convention.

Values in kJ.

State functions: only the start and the end count

A state function has a value that depends only on the present state of the system (its temperature, pressure, amount, …), not on how it got there. Your altitude is a state function: walking from Zurich to the top of the Uetliberg changes it by the same amount whichever path you take. The distance you walk is not.

\(E\) is a state function, so \(\Delta E\) depends only on the initial and final states. But \(q\) and \(w\) are not state functions: how the energy change is split between heat and work depends on the path. Brown's example is a battery. Short-circuit it with a wire, and all its energy comes out as heat. Let it drive a motor, and part comes out as work. The battery ends up just as flat either way.

Illustrative numbers: a battery that loses 100 kJ.

2 · Enthalpy and pressure–volume work

Most chemistry happens in open flasks, at the constant pressure of the atmosphere. If a reaction makes gas, the gas has to push the atmosphere back to make room. That costs work. Picture a gas in a cylinder pushing a piston of area \(A\) a distance \(\Delta h\) against a constant pressure \(P\):

\[ w = -F\,\Delta h = -P A\,\Delta h = -P\,\Delta V \]

The minus sign: if the volume grows (\(\Delta V > 0\)), the system does work, so it loses energy and \(w < 0\).

To keep the bookkeeping simple we define a new state function, the enthalpy:

\[ H \equiv E + PV \]

At constant pressure the change is \(\Delta H = \Delta E + P\Delta V = (q_p - P\Delta V) + P\Delta V\), so

\[ \Delta H = q_p \qquad \text{(constant pressure, only } P\text{–}V \text{ work)} \]

The enthalpy change is simply the heat that flows at constant pressure, and heat is easy to measure (section 4). \(\Delta H > 0\): endothermic, heat flows in. \(\Delta H < 0\): exothermic, heat flows out. Wait: \(H\) is a state function but \(q\) is not? No contradiction. \(q_p\) is the heat for one particular kind of path: constant pressure, no work other than \(P\)–\(V\) work. In a sealed rigid vessel (constant volume) no \(P\)–\(V\) work is done, so there \(\Delta E = q_V\).

The work comes only from the change in the amount of gas: for ideal gases at constant \(T\) and \(P\), \(P\Delta V = \Delta n_\text{gas} RT\), about 2.48 kJ per mole of gas at 25 °C. Liquids and solids hardly change volume. The reaction enthalpies are from Brown, Appendix C.

3 · Enthalpies of reaction

The enthalpy change of a reaction is \(\Delta H_\text{rxn} = H_\text{products} - H_\text{reactants}\). Written next to a balanced equation, it makes a thermochemical equation:

\[ 2\,\mathrm{H_2(g)} + \mathrm{O_2(g)} \longrightarrow 2\,\mathrm{H_2O(g)} \qquad \Delta H = -483.6\ \mathrm{kJ} \]

It reads: when 2 mol H₂ react with 1 mol O₂ to give 2 mol of water vapour, 483.6 kJ of heat are released. Three rules follow from \(H\) being a state function:

  1. Enthalpy is extensive. Double the amounts, double \(\Delta H\).
  2. Reverse the reaction, flip the sign: \(\Delta H_\text{reverse} = -\Delta H_\text{forward}\).
  3. States matter. Making liquid water instead of vapour releases more heat, because the vapour also gives off its heat of condensation.

\(\Delta H\) for 2 mol H₂: −483.6 kJ to vapour and −571.7 kJ to liquid (from Brown, Appendix C). The difference, 88.0 kJ, is the heat given off when 2 mol of water vapour condense at 25 °C.

Other named enthalpy changes work the same way, e.g. the heat of fusion \(\Delta H_\text{fus}\) (melting) and the heat of vaporization \(\Delta H_\text{vap}\) (boiling). Both are positive: you must add heat to melt or boil something. For water, \(\Delta H_\text{fus} = 6.01\) kJ/mol and \(\Delta H_\text{vap} = 40.7\) kJ/mol at 100 °C. 4 · Calorimetry: measuring heat with a thermometer

Heat flowing into something raises its temperature. How much depends on its heat capacity: the heat needed to warm it by 1 K (which is the same as 1 °C).

\[ q = m\,c_s\,\Delta T \qquad\text{or}\qquad q = n\,C_m\,\Delta T \]

\(c_s\) is the specific heat capacity (J g⁻¹ K⁻¹), \(C_m\) the molar heat capacity (J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹), and \(\Delta T = T_\text{final} - T_\text{initial}\). Check the sign: if the temperature rises, \(\Delta T > 0\) and \(q > 0\): heat went in.

Water is unusual. At 4.18 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹ it has one of the largest specific heats of any common substance, about 10 times that of iron. The same heat that warms a kilogram of water by 1 °C warms a kilogram of iron by about 9 °C. The oceans store huge amounts of heat with little change in temperature, which keeps coastal climates mild and slows down climate change.

The coffee-cup calorimeter

Drop a hot piece of metal into water in an insulated cup. Heat flows from the metal to the water (and the cup) until they are at the same temperature. No heat leaves the cup, so the heat lost by one is the heat gained by the other:

\[ q_\text{metal} + q_\text{water} + q_\text{cup} = 0 \]
metalwater and cup
  1. Use the same mass and temperatures for aluminium and gold. Which one warms the water more, and why?
  2. Give the cup a heat capacity. The final temperature drops: part of the heat warms the cup instead of the water.
  3. Swap the temperatures (cold metal, warm water). All the signs flip.

Measuring a reaction

Coffee cup: constant pressure

Run the reaction in water in an open, insulated cup. The reaction is the system, the water the surroundings. Heat released by the reaction warms the water: \(q_\text{rxn} = -q_\text{soln} = -m\,c_s\,\Delta T\). Because the pressure is constant, \(q_\text{rxn} = \Delta H\). Exothermic reaction: \(q_\text{rxn} < 0\) and the water warms up.

Bomb calorimeter: constant volume

Burn the sample with oxygen in a sealed steel "bomb" sitting in water. The volume cannot change, so no \(P\)–\(V\) work is done and the heat measured is \(\Delta E\): \(q_\text{rxn} = -C_\text{cal}\,\Delta T = \Delta E\). For most reactions \(\Delta E\) and \(\Delta H\) differ only by a few kJ (section 2).

5 · Hess's law: add up the steps

Because \(H\) is a state function, \(\Delta H\) does not care how you get from reactants to products. If a reaction can be written as a sum of steps, its enthalpy change is the sum of theirs:

\[ \Delta H_\text{rxn} = \Delta H_1 + \Delta H_2 + \Delta H_3 + \cdots \]

This lets you find \(\Delta H\) for reactions that are hard or impossible to measure, such as turning graphite into diamond. Use the three rules from section 3: reverse a step and flip its sign; multiply a step and multiply its \(\Delta H\).

6 · Enthalpies of formation

Instead of tabulating \(\Delta H\) for every possible reaction, chemists tabulate one number per substance. The standard enthalpy of formation \(\Delta H_f^\circ\) is the enthalpy change for making 1 mol of a substance from its elements, everything in its standard state (the pure substance at 1 atm and, usually, 25 °C):

\[ 3\,\mathrm{C(s,\ graphite)} + 4\,\mathrm{H_2(g)} \longrightarrow \mathrm{C_3H_8(g)} \qquad \Delta H_f^\circ = -103.85\ \mathrm{kJ/mol} \]

An element in its most stable form (O₂(g), C(graphite), Fe(s), Br₂(l), …) has \(\Delta H_f^\circ = 0\): there is nothing to form. Then any reaction can be done in two imaginary steps (Hess's law): take the reactants apart into elements, then build the products from them:

\[ \Delta H^\circ_\text{rxn} = \sum_\text{products} n\,\Delta H_f^\circ - \sum_\text{reactants} m\,\Delta H_f^\circ \]

\(n\) and \(m\) are the coefficients in the balanced equation. Look up \(\Delta H_f^\circ\) in Brown, Appendix C.

  3. Form 4 H₂O(l): \(4\,\mathrm{H_2(g)} + 2\,\mathrm{O_2(g)} \to 4\,\mathrm{H_2O(l)}\), \(\Delta H_3 = 4 \times (-285.83) = -1143.3\) kJ.
\[ \Delta H^\circ_\text{rxn} = 103.85 - 1180.5 - 1143.3 = -2220.0\ \mathrm{kJ} \]

O₂ is an element in its standard state, so it adds nothing. The units: kJ for the reaction as written, often written kJ/mol meaning "per mole of reaction".
