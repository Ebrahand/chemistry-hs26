2 · The rate law: how does the rate depend on concentration?

For a reaction \(a\mathrm{A} + b\mathrm{B} \rightarrow \text{products}\), the rate usually depends on the concentrations of the reactants like this:

\[ \text{rate} = k[\mathrm{A}]^m[\mathrm{B}]^n \]
  • \(m\) is the order in A and \(n\) the order in B. Their sum \(m + n\) is the overall order.
  • \(k\) is the rate constant. It depends on temperature, but not on concentration.
  • The orders are measured. In general they are not the coefficients \(a\) and \(b\) of the balanced equation.

What does an order mean?

The order tells you how strongly the rate reacts when you change one concentration. Pick a rate law, then change the concentrations and see what happens to the rate.

The rule to remember: multiplying a concentration by \(x\) multiplies the rate by \(x^{\text{order}}\). Order 0: no effect. Order 1: the rate changes in proportion. Order 2: doubling the concentration makes the reaction four times faster.

Order is not stoichiometry

You cannot read a rate law off the balanced equation. Some real examples (gas-phase reactions, from Brown, Ch. 14):

ReactionMeasured rate lawWhat it shows
\(2\,\mathrm{N_2O_5} \rightarrow 4\,\mathrm{NO_2} + \mathrm{O_2}\)\(\text{rate} = k[\mathrm{N_2O_5}]\) Coefficient 2, but first order.

Why the orders come out like this is a question about the reaction mechanism, the individual steps the reaction goes through. That comes later in the kinetics chapter.

3 · How do zero-, first- and second-order reactions change in time?

Take a reaction \(\mathrm{A} \rightarrow \text{products}\). The rate law tells you how fast A disappears right now, given how much A is left:

\[ \text{rate} = -\frac{d[\mathrm{A}]}{dt} = k[\mathrm{A}]^n \]

This is a rule for the slope of the [A]-versus-time curve. If you follow that rule moment by moment, you get the whole curve. The order \(n\) decides what the curve looks like:

  • Zero order (\(n = 0\)): the slope never changes. The same amount of A disappears every second, until none is left.
  • First order (\(n = 1\)): the slope is proportional to [A]. The same fraction of A disappears every second.
  • Second order (\(n = 2\)): the slope is proportional to [A]². When half of A is left, the reaction goes four times slower, so it drags on for a long time.

Why does k have different units for each order?

A rate is always a concentration per time, \(\mathrm{M\,s^{-1}}\). In \(\text{rate} = k[\mathrm{A}]^n\) the factor \([\mathrm{A}]^n\) carries \(\mathrm{M}^n\), so \(k\) has to make up the difference:

\[ k \;:\; \mathrm{M}^{1-n}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1} \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad n=0:\ \mathrm{M\,s^{-1}}, \quad n=1:\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}, \quad n=2:\ \mathrm{M^{-1}\,s^{-1}} \]

That is why the three sliders can show the same number, 0.050, and still describe three very different reactions. You cannot compare k values of different orders directly.

Where do the integrated rate laws come from? A short look at the ODE

The rate law is an ordinary differential equation: it links [A] to its own derivative. We solve it by separation of variables: put everything with [A] on one side and everything with t on the other, then integrate from the start (\(t = 0\), \([\mathrm{A}]_0\)) to time \(t\).

First order.

\[ -\frac{d[\mathrm{A}]}{dt} = k[\mathrm{A}] \;\Rightarrow\; \int_{[\mathrm{A}]_0}^{[\mathrm{A}]_t} \frac{d[\mathrm{A}]}{[\mathrm{A}]} = -k\int_0^t dt \;\Rightarrow\; \ln[\mathrm{A}]_t - \ln[\mathrm{A}]_0 = -kt \]

Zero order.

\[ -\frac{d[\mathrm{A}]}{dt} = k \;\Rightarrow\; \int_{[\mathrm{A}]_0}^{[\mathrm{A}]_t} d[\mathrm{A}] = -k\int_0^t dt \;\Rightarrow\; [\mathrm{A}]_t = -kt + [\mathrm{A}]_0 \]

Second order.

\[ -\frac{d[\mathrm{A}]}{dt} = k[\mathrm{A}]^2 \;\Rightarrow\; \int_{[\mathrm{A}]_0}^{[\mathrm{A}]_t} \frac{d[\mathrm{A}]}{[\mathrm{A}]^2} = -k\int_0^t dt \;\Rightarrow\; \frac{1}{[\mathrm{A}]_t} = kt + \frac{1}{[\mathrm{A}]_0} \]

Half-life. Put \([\mathrm{A}]_t = \tfrac12[\mathrm{A}]_0\) and solve for \(t\):

\[ t_{1/2} = \frac{[\mathrm{A}]_0}{2k}\ \ (n=0), \qquad t_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 2}{k} = \frac{0.693}{k}\ \ (n=1), \qquad t_{1/2} = \frac{1}{k[\mathrm{A}]_0}\ \ (n=2) \]

Only the first-order half-life does not contain \([\mathrm{A}]_0\). That is why it stays constant as the reaction goes on. Module 4 (coming soon) will let you watch a computer solve the ODE step by step.
