1 · Why do things dissolve?

A solution is a homogeneous mixture: a solute spread out evenly in a solvent. Whether a solute dissolves depends on two things you already know:

  • Enthalpy (week 4): which intermolecular forces are broken and which are formed (week 8).
  • Entropy (week 5): mixing spreads the particles over more arrangements, so entropy almost always favours dissolving.

Think of dissolving as three steps. Because enthalpy is a state function, the order does not matter (Hess's law):

\[ \Delta H_\text{soln} = \underbrace{\Delta H_1}_{\text{separate solute particles}\ (>0)} + \underbrace{\Delta H_2}_{\text{make room in the solvent}\ (>0)} + \underbrace{\Delta H_3}_{\text{solute–solvent attractions form}\ (<0)} \]

Illustrative units, not real kJ/mol. Real numbers for NaCl are below.

Real numbers for NaCl in water. Pulling the ions out of the crystal costs the lattice energy, +788 kJ/mol. Surrounding the free ions with water molecules (ion–dipole forces, steps 2 and 3 together, the hydration) gives back −784 kJ/mol. The net \(\Delta H_\text{soln}\) is only about +4 kJ/mol, the small difference of two huge numbers. Entropy does the rest.

Solute in water\(\Delta H_\text{soln}\) (kJ/mol)What you notice
NaOH−44.5the solution gets hot
NaCl+3.9hardly any temperature change
NH₄NO₃+25.7the solution gets cold: instant cold packs

NH₄NO₃ dissolves although it is endothermic: the entropy gain makes \(\Delta G = \Delta H - T\Delta S\) negative (week 5). Values at 25 °C for dilute solutions.

“Like dissolves like”

A solute dissolves well when the new solute–solvent attractions are about as strong as the solute–solute and solvent–solvent attractions they replace. Polar and ionic substances dissolve in polar solvents (water). Non-polar substances dissolve in non-polar solvents (hexane, oil). Oil does not mix with water because the water molecules attract each other through hydrogen bonds much more strongly than they attract oil molecules.

The alcohols show this step by step. The O–H group likes water; the hydrocarbon chain likes hexane. As the chain gets longer, the molecule looks more and more like a hydrocarbon:

2 · Solubility: pressure and temperature

A saturated solution is in dynamic equilibrium with undissolved solute: particles dissolve and come back out at the same rate. Its concentration is the solubility.

Gases: Henry's law

Press harder on a gas above a liquid and more gas molecules hit the surface and dissolve, until the dissolved concentration is high enough for the rates in and out to be equal again. The solubility of a gas is proportional to its partial pressure:

\[ S_\text{g} = k\,P_\text{g} \]

\(S_\text{g}\) is the solubility (mol/L), \(P_\text{g}\) the partial pressure of that gas above the solution (atm), and \(k\) the Henry's-law constant (mol L⁻¹ atm⁻¹), which depends on the gas, the solvent and the temperature.

Gas (in water, 25 °C)

The picture shows equal volumes of gas and liquid, with the number of molecules in proportion to the real concentrations. At 25 °C, a litre of water holds about 0.8 times as much CO₂ as a litre of the gas above it, but only 0.03 times as much O₂. Henry's-law constants at 25 °C: CO₂ 3.4 × 10⁻², O₂ 1.3 × 10⁻³ mol L⁻¹ atm⁻¹.

Temperature

Gases become less soluble when the water gets warmer. Dissolving a gas releases heat, and the faster-moving gas molecules escape more easily. A warm soda goes flat faster, and fish in a warm lake or near the cooling-water outlet of a power plant can run short of oxygen.

Most solids become more soluble at higher temperature, which is why sugar dissolves better in hot tea. There are exceptions, for example Ce₂(SO₄)₃, which gets less soluble.

3 · Concentration units: same solution, five numbers

One solution, described five ways. Each unit is a ratio of an amount of solute to an amount of something else:

4 · Colligative properties: only the number of particles counts

Some properties of a solution depend only on how many solute particles there are, not on what they are. These colligative properties are vapour-pressure lowering, boiling-point elevation, freezing-point depression and osmotic pressure. All four start from the same idea: dissolved particles make the solvent less eager to leave the solution.

Vapour-pressure lowering: Raoult's law

\[ P_\text{A} = X_\text{A}\,P^\circ_\text{A} \]

The partial pressure of the solvent A above the solution is its mole fraction times the vapour pressure of the pure solvent. In an ideal solution each solvent molecule leaves the liquid just as easily as in the pure solvent, but only a fraction \(X_\text{A}\) of the molecules are solvent. A non-volatile solute (sugar, salt) does not contribute to the vapour at all.

Mixture
Is the solute really “blocking the surface”?

That picture is popular but not quite right. The deeper reason is entropy (week 5): the solvent in a solution is mixed with solute particles and so has more possible arrangements than the pure solvent. Leaving the solution to become vapour gives up that extra entropy, so fewer molecules leave. Real solutions follow Raoult's law well when they are dilute and when solute and solvent attract each other about as much as they attract themselves.

Why the boiling point goes up and the freezing point goes down

Boiling: the liquid has to reach a vapour pressure of 1 atm. The solution's vapour pressure is lower at every temperature, so it has to get hotter. Freezing: pure ice forms (the solute stays in the liquid). The freezing point is where the liquid and the ice have the same vapour pressure. Zoom in on

The formulas, for any solvent

\[ \Delta T_b = i\,K_b\,m \qquad\qquad \Delta T_f = i\,K_f\,m \]

\(m\) is the molality of the solute, \(K_b\) and \(K_f\) are constants of the solvent (°C/m), and \(i\) is the van 't Hoff factor: the number of particles each formula unit gives in solution. Both \(\Delta T\) are positive numbers: the boiling point goes up by \(\Delta T_b\), the freezing point goes down by \(\Delta T_f\).

Real van 't Hoff factors are a bit smaller

Oppositely charged ions attract each other in solution. Some of the time they move around as a pair and count as one particle, so the measured \(i\) is smaller than the ideal one. The more dilute the solution and the smaller the ionic charges, the closer \(i\) gets to the ideal value:

Osmosis

A semipermeable membrane lets solvent molecules through but not the solute. Put pure water on one side and a solution on the other: water flows into the solution, the same way it "wants" to dilute any solution. The pressure you must apply to the solution to stop this flow is the osmotic pressure:

\[ \Pi = i\,M R T \]

with \(M\) the molarity. Watch the units: \(M\) in mol/L with \(R = 0.08206\ \mathrm{L\,atm\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}\) gives atm; \(M\) in mol/m³ with \(R = 8.314\ \mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}\) gives Pa. Push harder than \(\Pi\) and water flows the other way: reverse osmosis, which is how seawater is desalinated.

Solute

Your red blood cells do this all the time

The inside of a red blood cell is about 0.30 mol of particles per litre. In a solution with the same particle concentration (isotonic, e.g. 0.9 % NaCl, the "saline drip") nothing happens. In pure water (hypotonic) water flows in and the cell can burst. In salty water (hypertonic) water flows out and the cell shrivels. Move the concentration slider above and watch the cell.

Getting a molar mass from a colligative property

Measure \(\Delta T_f\), \(\Delta T_b\) or \(\Pi\), work out the amount of solute in mol, and divide the mass by it. Example: 1.00 g of a protein is dissolved in water to make 100.0 mL of solution. At 25 °C the osmotic pressure is 0.248 kPa.

\[ n = \frac{\Pi V}{RT} = \frac{248\ \mathrm{Pa} \times 1.000\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{m^3}}{8.314\ \mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}K^{-1}} \times 298.15\ \mathrm{K}} = 1.00\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{mol} \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad \mathcal{M} = \frac{1.00\ \mathrm{g}}{1.00\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{mol}} = 1.00\times10^{5}\ \mathrm{g/mol} \]

The freezing point of the same solution would drop by only about 0.0002 °C, far too little to measure. That is why osmotic pressure is used for large molecules such as proteins and polymers.

5 · Colloids

Between a true solution (particles below about 1 nm) and a suspension that settles out lies the colloid: particles of roughly 1 to 1000 nm that stay dispersed. They are too small to see or to settle, but big enough to scatter light. A beam of light through a colloid is visible from the side (the Tyndall effect); through a true solution it is not. Think of a sunbeam in a dusty room, or car headlights in fog.

Rows: what is dispersed. Columns: what it is dispersed in.

In water, hydrophilic colloids (many proteins) are stabilised by polar or charged groups on their surface. Hydrophobic ones (oil droplets) need help: an emulsifying agent such as soap or the lecithin in egg yolk has a polar head and a long non-polar tail. The tails dissolve in the oil droplet, the heads face the water, and the droplet stays suspended. This is how soap washes grease away. Adding salt or heating can make colloid particles clump together and settle (coagulation).
