1 · Light comes in packets

Light is an electromagnetic wave. Its wavelength \(\lambda\) and frequency \(\nu\) are tied together by the speed of light, which is the same for all colours:

\[ c = \lambda\,\nu, \qquad c = 2.998 \times 10^{8}\ \mathrm{m/s} \]

But light does not deliver its energy continuously. It comes in packets, the photons, and each photon carries an energy set only by its frequency:

\[ E = h\nu = \frac{hc}{\lambda}, \qquad h = 6.626 \times 10^{-34}\ \mathrm{J\,s}\ \text{(Planck's constant)} \]

Short wavelength = high frequency = energetic photons. A brighter lamp sends more photons, not stronger ones. That is why UV light gives you a sunburn and a much brighter red lamp does not: one UV photon has enough energy to damage a molecule, one red photon does not, however many of them there are.

  1. Drag across the visible part. Colour follows wavelength: about 430 nm is blue, 530 nm green, 630 nm red. Visible light runs from about 400 to 750 nm.
  2. Compare the energy per mole of photons with the energy of a C–C bond, 348 kJ/mol (week 7). Where do photons become strong enough to break bonds?

Units: watch the nanometres

Put \(\lambda\) in metres into \(E = hc/\lambda\): 413 nm = 413 × 10⁻⁹ m = 4.13 × 10⁻⁷ m. Then \(E = (6.626\times10^{-34})(2.998\times10^{8})/(4.13\times10^{-7}) = 4.81\times10^{-19}\) J for one photon. For a mole of photons multiply by \(N_A = 6.022\times10^{23}\ \mathrm{mol^{-1}}\): 290 kJ/mol.

2 · The hydrogen atom

A hot solid glows in all colours. Hot hydrogen gas does not: it emits light at a few sharp wavelengths only, its line spectrum. In the visible part there are four lines:

Johann Balmer (a Swiss school teacher) and later Johannes Rydberg found a formula that fits every line, the Rydberg equation, with whole numbers \(n_2 > n_1 > 0\):

\[ \frac{1}{\lambda} = R_H\left(\frac{1}{n_1^2} - \frac{1}{n_2^2}\right), \qquad R_H = 1.097 \times 10^{7}\ \mathrm{m^{-1}} \]

Bohr's explanation: only certain energies are allowed

Niels Bohr proposed that the electron can only have certain energies, labelled by \(n = 1, 2, 3, \dots\) The zero of energy is an electron that has left the atom (\(n = \infty\)), so all bound energies are negative:

\[ E_n = -hcR_H\,\frac{1}{n^2} = \left(-2.18 \times 10^{-18}\ \mathrm{J}\right)\frac{1}{n^2} \]

When the electron drops from a higher level to a lower one, the energy difference leaves as one photon: \(\Delta E = E_\text{f} - E_\text{i} < 0\) and \(E_\text{photon} = -\Delta E = hc/\lambda\). To go up, the atom has to absorb a photon of exactly the right energy.

How to find the transition without guessing

Only lines that end on \(n_\text{f} = 1\) have enough energy for the UV below 122 nm, so take \(n_\text{f} = 1\). The photon energy is \(hc/\lambda = 2.09\times10^{-18}\) J, which must equal \(2.18\times10^{-18}\,\mathrm{J}\,(1/1^2 - 1/n_\text{i}^2)\). So \(1/n_\text{i}^2 = 1 - 0.959 = 0.041\) and \(n_\text{i} \approx 4.96\). Quantum numbers are whole numbers: \(n_\text{i} = 5\).

What Bohr got right, and what he got wrong

Right: the energy of the electron is quantized, and the levels \(E_n \propto -1/n^2\) are correct for hydrogen. Wrong: the electron does not circle the nucleus on a fixed orbit, and the model fails for every atom with more than one electron. Quantum mechanics fixes both (section 3).

Why there are no orbits: the uncertainty principle

Heisenberg: you cannot know both the position \(x\) and the momentum \(p = mv\) of a particle exactly. The uncertainties are linked:

\[ \Delta x \cdot \Delta(mv) \ \ge\ \frac{h}{4\pi} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \Delta x \ \ge\ \frac{h}{4\pi\, m\, \Delta v} \]

Note that it is the uncertainty in the speed, \(\Delta v\), that matters, not the speed itself. Try it for a few objects:

Object

So for an electron in an atom we can only give probabilities for where it is: a fuzzy electron cloud instead of a sharp orbit. For a mosquito the uncertainty is far below the size of a nucleus, which is why everyday objects behave classically.

Matter waves (Brown 6.4)

Louis de Broglie suggested that if light (a wave) behaves like particles, particles might behave like waves, with wavelength \(\lambda = h/(mv)\). For an electron moving at \(2.2\times10^{6}\) m/s this is 0.33 nm, about the size of an atom, so wave behaviour matters. For a 45 g golf ball at 40 m/s it is \(3.7\times10^{-34}\) m: far too small to ever notice. Electron microscopes use the short wavelength of fast electrons to see details smaller than visible light can show.

3 · Orbitals and quantum numbers

Quantum mechanics describes the electron by a wavefunction \(\psi\). Its square, \(\psi^2\), is the probability density: how likely you are to find the electron at a given place. A solution with a definite energy is called an orbital. Each orbital is labelled by three quantum numbers, and each electron in it by a fourth:

SymbolNameAllowed valuesTells you
\(n\)principal (the shell)1, 2, 3, …size and energy; the same \(n\) as Bohr's
\(\ell\)angular momentum (the subshell)0, 1, …, \(n-1\)shape: \(\ell\) = 0, 1, 2, 3 is s, p, d, f
\(m_\ell\)magnetic\(-\ell, \dots, 0, \dots, +\ell\)orientation in space; \(2\ell+1\) orbitals per subshell
\(m_s\)spin magnetic\(+\tfrac12\) or \(-\tfrac12\)spin "up" or "down" (the electron behaves like a tiny magnet)

4 · Filling the orbitals

In hydrogen, the energy depends only on \(n\): 2s and 2p have the same energy. With more electrons, the electrons repel each other and the subshells split: for a given \(n\), s < p < d < f. The reason is screening (section 5): an s electron spends some time very close to the nucleus, where the inner electrons do not shield it, so it is held more tightly.

To build the ground state of an atom, fill the orbitals from the bottom up with three rules:

Aufbau

Fill the subshells in order of energy. The periodic table gives the order: 1s 2s 2p 3s 3p 4s 3d 4p 5s 4d 5p 6s 4f 5d 6p.

Pauli exclusion

No two electrons have the same four quantum numbers. So an orbital (a box) holds at most two electrons, with opposite spins: s 2, p 6, d 10, f 14.

Hund's rule

In a subshell with several orbitals, put one electron in each box first, all with parallel spins, before pairing them up. Electrons avoid each other when they can.

5 · Periodic trends

Screening and effective nuclear charge

A valence electron is attracted by the nucleus (charge \(+Z\)) but repelled by the inner, core electrons. The core electrons screen the nucleus, so the valence electron feels only an effective nuclear charge

\[ Z_\text{eff} = Z - S \]

where \(S\) is roughly the number of core electrons. Sodium: \(Z = 11\), 10 core electrons, so the 3s electron feels about \(+1\).

  • Across a period \(Z\) goes up by one each step but the core stays the same, so \(Z_\text{eff}\) increases.
  • Down a group each new shell adds as many core electrons as protons, so \(Z_\text{eff}\) stays about the same (it rises slightly), but the valence electrons are in a bigger shell.

The two rules behind every trend
  • Down a group: the valence electrons are in a higher shell \(n\), further from the nucleus. Atoms get bigger, and their outer electrons are easier to remove.
  • Across a period (left to right): same shell, but larger \(Z_\text{eff}\). The electrons are pulled in harder: atoms get smaller, and their electrons are harder to remove.

Ionization energy and electron affinity

Ionization energy \(I\): the energy needed to remove an electron from a gaseous atom or ion.

\[ \mathrm{Na}(g) \rightarrow \mathrm{Na^+}(g) + e^- \qquad I_1 = +496\ \mathrm{kJ/mol} \]

It always costs energy, so \(I > 0\) (endothermic). Each further electron is harder to remove than the last: \(I_1 < I_2 < I_3 < \dots\)

Electron affinity \(EA\): the energy change when a gaseous atom takes up an electron.

\[ \mathrm{Cl}(g) + e^- \rightarrow \mathrm{Cl^-}(g) \qquad EA = -349\ \mathrm{kJ/mol} \]

Negative means energy is released: chlorine "likes" the extra electron. Halogens have \(EA \ll 0\). Noble gases have \(EA > 0\): their next electron would have to go into a new shell.

Sign convention: we use Brown's and the lecture's, \(EA\) = energy change. Some other books define the electron affinity as the energy released, with the opposite sign.

Ions: cations shrink, anions grow

A cation is smaller than its parent atom: it has lost electrons (often its whole outer shell), and the remaining ones are pulled in harder. An anion is larger: the extra electron increases the repulsion between the electrons, and the cloud spreads out. For ions with the same number of electrons (an isoelectronic series), more protons means a smaller ion.
