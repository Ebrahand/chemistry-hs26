Atoms, molecules and reactions: the language of chemistry

Chemistry has its own alphabet (atoms and elements), words (molecules and compounds) and sentences (reactions). This week you learn to read and write it, and to count atoms you cannot see by weighing them. Brown, Chapters 1–3.

After this week you should be able to
  • tell elements, compounds and mixtures apart, and homogeneous from heterogeneous mixtures
  • convert units with conversion factors, and report a result with the right number of significant figures
  • read a nuclide symbol such as \(^{58}_{28}\mathrm{Ni}^{2+}\): protons, neutrons, electrons
  • calculate an atomic weight from isotope masses and abundances
  • write the formula and name of an ionic compound from its ions
  • balance a chemical equation
  • convert between grams, moles and number of particles, and find the limiting reactant and the yield

1 · Matter and measurement

Chemistry is the study of the properties and behaviour of matter. Lecture 1 sorted matter like a language:

ChemistryLanguageMeaning
Atomletterthe smallest unit of an element
Elementlettera substance made of only one type of atom (O₂, Fe, He)
Moleculewordtwo or more atoms joined together (O₂, H₂O)
Compoundwordmade of two or more different elements, in a fixed ratio (H₂O, NaCl)
Reactionsentencedescribes a chemical change from one substance to another

Elements and compounds are pure substances: their composition and properties are the same in every sample. A mixture contains two or more pure substances in a ratio that can vary. If the mixture looks the same everywhere it is homogeneous (mineral water, air, brass), otherwise heterogeneous (milk, sand in water, granite). Pure water is a pure substance.

A physical change (melting ice, dissolving sugar) does not change what the substances are. A chemical change (burning, rusting) makes new substances. Every property is also either extensive, depending on how much you have (mass, volume), or intensive, not depending on it (temperature, density, melting point). Intensive properties identify a substance.

SI units

Science uses the SI system: seven base units (m, kg, s, K, mol, A, cd), with prefixes for big and small numbers. Everything else is derived from them: \(1\ \mathrm{J} = 1\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2\,s^{-2}}\), \(1\ \mathrm{Pa} = 1\ \mathrm{N\,m^{-2}}\). Temperature in kelvin: \(T(\mathrm{K}) = T(^\circ\mathrm{C}) + 273.15\). A litre is not SI but used everywhere: \(1\ \mathrm{L} = 1\ \mathrm{dm^3} = 1000\ \mathrm{mL}\) and \(1\ \mathrm{mL} = 1\ \mathrm{cm^3}\).

Dimensional analysis: let the units do the work

A conversion factor is a fraction whose top and bottom are the same quantity in different units, so it equals 1. \(1\ \mathrm{cal} = 4.184\ \mathrm{J}\) gives two of them: \(\frac{4.184\ \mathrm{J}}{1\ \mathrm{cal}}\) and \(\frac{1\ \mathrm{cal}}{4.184\ \mathrm{J}}\). Pick the one that cancels the unit you have and leaves the unit you want. If a factor is upside down, the units do not cancel and you see it at once. (NASA lost the Mars Climate Orbiter in 1999 to a units mix-up.)

Significant figures

Every measurement has an uncertainty, and the last digit you write is the uncertain one. A measurement can be precise (repeat measurements agree) without being accurate (close to the true value), and the other way round. Exact numbers, such as 12 eggs in a dozen or the 2 in \(2\,\mathrm{H_2}\), have no uncertainty.

The rules for zeros

  • Non-zero digits are always significant.
  • Zeros between non-zero digits are significant: 60023 has 5.
  • Zeros at the start are never significant: 0.006 has 1. They only place the decimal point.
  • Zeros at the end count if there is a decimal point: 0.0060 has 2, 6.0 has 2. Without one, 6000 is ambiguous (taken as 1). Write 6.0 × 10³ to say you mean 2.

In calculations

  • × and ÷: as many significant figures as the factor with the fewest.
  • + and −: as many decimal places as the number with the fewest.
  • Several steps: keep all digits in the calculator and round only at the end.
  • Logarithms: decimal places of the log = significant figures of the number (matters for pH).

2 · Inside the atom

Dalton's atomic theory (1803–1807): each element is made of atoms; all atoms of one element are chemically identical; atoms are not created, destroyed or changed in a chemical reaction; compounds are combinations of atoms of two or more elements. This explains the laws of conservation of mass, constant composition and multiple proportions.

We now know atoms are made of three smaller particles:

ParticleChargeMassMass (u)Where
proton\(+e\)1.67 × 10⁻²⁷ kg1.0073nucleus
neutron01.67 × 10⁻²⁷ kg1.0087nucleus
electron\(-e\)9.11 × 10⁻³¹ kg5.486 × 10⁻⁴electron cloud

\(e = 1.60\times10^{-19}\) C. The nucleus is about \(10^{-4}\) Å across, the electron cloud 1–5 Å (1 Å = 10⁻¹⁰ m). If the electron cloud were a football pitch, the nucleus would be a 1 mm grain at the centre spot, yet it holds almost all the mass.

  • The atomic number \(Z\) = number of protons. It decides which element it is: every carbon atom in the universe has 6 protons. A neutral atom has as many electrons as protons.
  • The mass number \(A\) = protons + neutrons.
  • Isotopes of an element have the same \(Z\) but a different number of neutrons. They have almost the same chemistry (that is set by the electrons) but a different mass.

Atomic mass and atomic weight

Masses in kilograms are awkward for atoms, so chemists use the atomic mass unit: by definition a ¹²C atom has a mass of exactly 12 u, and \(1\ \mathrm{u} = 1.66054\times10^{-27}\ \mathrm{kg}\). The atomic weight on the periodic table is the average mass of the atoms of an element, weighted by how common each isotope is in nature:

\[ \text{atomic weight} = \sum_\text{isotopes} (\text{fractional abundance}) \times (\text{isotope mass}) \]

Carbon: \(0.9893 \times 12\ \mathrm{u} + 0.0107 \times 13.0034\ \mathrm{u} = 12.01\ \mathrm{u}\). No single carbon atom weighs 12.01 u.

Molecules

Only the noble gases exist as single atoms. Most matter is made of molecules or ions. A molecular formula gives the actual numbers of atoms (H₂O₂), the empirical formula only the smallest whole-number ratio (HO). Structural formulas show which atom is bonded to which; perspective drawings, ball-and-stick and space-filling models also show the 3D shape. You can rotate real 3D shapes on the week 7 page.

Ions and ionic compounds

An ion is an atom or molecule with a charge. Remove electrons and you get a positive cation (Na⁺); add electrons and you get a negative anion (Cl⁻). Atoms at the edges of the table gain or lose electrons until they have as many as the nearest noble gas: group 1 forms 1+, group 2 forms 2+, group 16 forms 2−, group 17 forms 1−. Polyatomic ions are groups of atoms with an overall charge, such as ammonium NH₄⁺ and sulfate SO₄²⁻.

An ionic compound forms between cations and anions, usually a metal and a nonmetal. The compound is electrically neutral, so the charges must cancel. Its formula is an empirical formula: solid NaCl is a 3D lattice of Na⁺ and Cl⁻, not NaCl molecules.

Naming rules (Brown 2.8)
  • Cations: metal name (sodium ion). If a metal can have more than one charge, give it as a Roman numeral: iron(II) Fe²⁺, iron(III) Fe³⁺. Older names use -ous (lower charge) and -ic (higher): ferrous, ferric. Cations of nonmetals end in -ium: ammonium NH₄⁺.
  • Monatomic anions: -ide: chloride Cl⁻, oxide O²⁻, nitride N³⁻. Also hydroxide OH⁻ and cyanide CN⁻.
  • Oxyanions: -ate for the common one, -ite for one O fewer (same charge): nitrate NO₃⁻, nitrite NO₂⁻; sulfate SO₄²⁻, sulfite SO₃²⁻. With four in a series: per…ate, …ate, …ite, hypo…ite (ClO₄⁻, ClO₃⁻, ClO₂⁻, ClO⁻). Adding H⁺ gives “hydrogen”: HCO₃⁻ hydrogen carbonate.
  • Ionic compound: cation name, then anion name: calcium chloride CaCl₂.
  • Acids: -ide → hydro…ic acid (HCl hydrochloric acid); -ate → -ic acid (HNO₃ nitric acid); -ite → -ous acid (HNO₂ nitrous acid).
  • Binary molecular compounds (two nonmetals): Greek prefixes mono-, di-, tri-, tetra-, penta-, hexa-: N₂O₄ dinitrogen tetroxide, CO carbon monoxide, SF₆ sulfur hexafluoride.

4 · Chemical equations

A chemical equation describes a reaction: \(\text{reactants} \longrightarrow \text{products}\). Atoms are not created or destroyed in a reaction, so every type of atom must be balanced: the same number on both sides. Balance by putting coefficients in front of the formulas. Never change the subscripts: that would turn H₂O into H₂O₂, a different substance.

\[ 2\,\mathrm{H_2} + \mathrm{O_2} \longrightarrow 2\,\mathrm{H_2O} \]

States are written in brackets: (s) solid, (l) liquid, (g) gas, (aq) dissolved in water. Some simple patterns come up again and again: combination (A + B → C, e.g. N₂ + 3 H₂ → 2 NH₃), decomposition (C → A + B, e.g. CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂ for cement) and combustion (burning in O₂; a hydrocarbon gives CO₂ and H₂O).

Atoms on each side (left: reactants, right: products)

Tip for combustion: balance C first, then H, and O last, because O₂ appears on its own. If you need a half O₂, multiply everything by 2. More practice with pictures: PhET Balancing Chemical Equations.

5 · The mole: counting by weighing

The formula weight (FW) is the sum of the atomic weights of all atoms in a formula: FW(H₂O) = 2 × 1.008 u + 16.00 u = 18.02 u, the mass of one water molecule. For a molecule this is also called the molecular weight \(M_w\); for an ionic solid it uses the empirical formula (NaCl, CaCl₂).

Lecture 1 worked out how many grams of O₂ react with 2.00 g of H₂ by counting molecules one by one: 6.02 × 10²³ of them, through u and kg. Tedious. The mole does that counting once and for all:

\[ 1\ \mathrm{mol} = N_A = 6.022\times10^{23}\ \text{items} \qquad \text{(Avogadro's number)} \]

\(N_A\) is chosen so that the atomic weight of any element in grams contains exactly one mole of atoms: one K atom weighs 39.0983 u, so 39.0983 g of potassium contain \(N_A\) atoms. The same holds for molecules. So the formula weight in u is numerically the molar mass \(M\) in g/mol, and you can read it off the periodic table. A mole is just a number, like a dozen (12): 6.02 × 10²³ marbles would cover the Earth in a layer about 5 km thick.

\[ n = \frac{m}{M} \qquad\qquad N = n \, N_A \]

Empirical formula from an analysis

A lab tells you the mass percentages of the elements in a compound. To get the formula: (1) take 100 g, so % becomes grams; (2) divide by the molar masses to get moles; (3) divide by the smallest number of moles; (4) multiply until all are whole numbers. If you also know the molar mass, the molecular formula is a whole-number multiple of the empirical one.

6 · Stoichiometry and limiting reactants

Stoichiometry is how much reactant is used up and how much product is made. The coefficients of a balanced equation are ratios of particles, and so of moles, never of grams: \(2\,\mathrm{H_2} + \mathrm{O_2} \to 2\,\mathrm{H_2O}\) says 2 mol H₂ react with 1 mol O₂. The route is always the same:

\[ \text{grams A} \xrightarrow{\ \div M_A\ } \text{mol A} \xrightarrow{\ \times\,\frac{\text{coeff. B}}{\text{coeff. A}}\ } \text{mol B} \xrightarrow{\ \times M_B\ } \text{grams B} \]

If you mix reactants in a ratio that is not the one in the equation, one runs out first: the limiting reactant. It decides how much product forms (the theoretical yield); the rest of the other reactant is left over (in excess). Think of sandwiches: 10 slices of bread and 3 slices of cheese make only 3 cheese sandwiches if each takes 2 bread and 1 cheese. The cheese limits, not because there is less of it, but because there is less of it per sandwich.

In the lab: grams

\[ \text{percent yield} = \frac{\text{actual yield}}{\text{theoretical yield}} \times 100\,\% \]

Real yields are below 100 %: side reactions, incomplete reactions, product lost while purifying. The lecture's D-MAVT example: hydrogen from electrolysis (2 H₂O → 2 H₂ + O₂) stored and burned in a gas turbine (2 H₂ + O₂ → 2 H₂O) for electricity when demand peaks.
